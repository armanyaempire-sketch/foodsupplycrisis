Australia is one of the clearest examples of why a disappointing crop forecast does not automatically mean a national food shortage.

ABARES expects Australia's 2026–27 winter crop to decline, but the forecast remains historically large.

At the same time, El Niño raises questions about rainfall, crop conditions and future grocery prices.

## What the latest crop forecast says

ABARES' September 2026 Australian Crop Report forecasts national winter crop production at 61 million tonnes, down 12% from the previous year.

Even after that decline, the crop would be 16% above the 10-year average and the fourth-largest on record if realized.

Wheat production is forecast to fall 17% to 29.9 million tonnes, which would still be slightly above the 10-year average.

That is a weaker crop, not an empty-food-shelves scenario.

## Why a lower harvest can still matter

Australia exports agricultural commodities.

A smaller exportable surplus can affect global buyers, particularly when several exporting countries are also experiencing weather or logistics problems.

Domestic prices do not necessarily move in exactly the same direction because Australia has its own production, trade and market conditions.

## El Niño's role

A stronger El Niño can affect rainfall and temperature patterns.

The impact varies across Australia.

Southern cropping regions can have different conditions from northern grain areas.

ABARES' current forecast reflects better-than-expected conditions in much of the south partly offset by poor conditions in parts of northern New South Wales and Queensland.

## What about grocery prices?

Food prices depend on much more than the harvest.

Retail prices include farm costs, processing, transport, labor, packaging, imports and retail margins.

A weaker wheat crop may matter more to flour and feed markets than directly to the price of every grocery item.

## Why Australia is not a simple shortage story

Australia remains a large agricultural producer with functioning domestic and international supply chains.

Its challenge is to maintain production and affordability while weather becomes less predictable and input costs remain elevated.

That is a very different problem from a nationwide disappearance of food.

## What to monitor

Watch:

- wheat and barley production;
- rainfall and soil moisture;
- export volumes;
- fertilizer prices;
- domestic grocery inflation;
- and El Niño updates.


## Why the exportable surplus matters more than the headline crop

ABARES’ national crop estimate is a production measure, not a direct estimate of grain available to overseas buyers. Domestic use, seed, feed, carry-in stocks, quality and logistics all affect the exportable surplus. A smaller harvest can therefore matter more to importers when it coincides with weak crops elsewhere, but the effect is not automatic.

For Australian households, the same crop number travels through a different channel. Wheat is only one input into bread and processed foods; milling, labour, packaging, transport, retail contracts and the exchange rate also matter. Track three separate series rather than treating them as one: the ABARES crop forecast, export shipment data and Australian consumer food-price data. A revision to the crop estimate should not be described as a supermarket shortage unless retail availability or prices provide evidence of that additional step.

## Why exporter status does not eliminate household price risk

Australia’s role in international agricultural markets can help it earn export revenue, but export strength does not insulate households from every grocery-price shock. Farm-gate prices, export prices and supermarket prices measure different stages of the food chain. Domestic retail costs also include labour, energy, packaging, processing, storage, transport and competition between retailers. Some products rely more on local production; others depend on imported ingredients or inputs.

| Question | Evidence to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Is a crop actually at risk? | Crop-specific seasonal outlook and measured conditions | A broad El Niño label is not a harvest result |
| Is the issue farm output or cost? | Production estimates alongside fuel, freight and input costs | Prices can rise even when output remains strong |
| Is the effect reaching consumers? | ABS food-price data and retail prices for the relevant category | Export prices do not map one-for-one to grocery prices |
| Is the pressure temporary? | Several monthly releases and revised crop estimates | A single month can reflect seasonality or a short-lived cost |
| Is supply concentrated? | Domestic production, imports and alternative suppliers | The effect differs by commodity and sourcing route |

For the local price picture, compare the [Australian Bureau of Statistics](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/price-indexes-and-inflation/consumer-price-index-australia) consumer-price data with [ABARES](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/abares/research-topics/agricultural-outlook) agricultural outlooks and the [Bureau of Meteorology](https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/enso/) ENSO information. These sources answer different questions: inflation describes prices paid, agricultural outlooks describe production and markets, and climate guidance describes risk rather than a confirmed yield change.

The key test is whether several pieces of evidence line up: a crop is exposed in a defined region and season, production estimates are changing, supply or input costs are moving, and retail data shows a corresponding consumer effect. If only the weather forecast changes, the correct wording is “risk” or “potential pressure,” not “food shortage.” Recheck the relevant crop when updated seasonal observations become available.

## Bottom line

Australia's 2026–27 outlook is weaker than the previous season but still historically strong.

The evidence supports a story about crop variability, farm costs and possible price pressure—not an imminent nationwide food shortage.
