> **Update — 13 November 2026:** This article now includes the September ABARES crop outlook, September consumer-price data and the Australian Government's published fertiliser-import exposure. Forecasts remain forecasts; the December ABARES update is the next major crop checkpoint.

## Key takeaways

- ABARES forecasts a 2026–27 winter crop of **61 million tonnes**, 12% below the previous season but still **16% above the 10-year average** and potentially the fourth-largest on record.
- The national total hides a sharp geographic split: southern growing regions have benefited from timely rain, while northern New South Wales and much of Queensland remain weaker.
- The nearer-term household issue is affordability and the cost of particular foods, not evidence of an imminent nationwide shortage. ABS recorded annual food and non-alcoholic beverage inflation of 3.2% in September.

## The crop numbers: a smaller year, not a failed harvest

ABARES's September 2026 Australian Crop Report estimates winter crop production at 61 million tonnes for the 2026–27 season. That is a 12% fall from the previous season, but the comparison needs context: the forecast remains 16% above the 10-year average through 2025–26. If achieved, it would be Australia's fourth-largest winter crop on record.

The headline is therefore not “Australia has lost 12% of its food”. It is that the country is moving down from a very large previous crop while remaining above its recent long-run production average. A year-on-year percentage change and the absolute level of output answer different questions.

| Indicator | Latest published figure | What it tells us |
|---|---:|---|
| Winter crop, 2026–27 | 61 Mt; −12% y/y | Still 16% above the 10-year average |
| Wheat | 29.9 Mt; −17% y/y | Still 3% above the 10-year average |
| Barley | 16.4 Mt; −3% y/y | 30% above the 10-year average |
| Canola | 7.3 Mt; −5% y/y | 37% above the 10-year average |
| Lentils | 2.3 Mt; +5% y/y | Second consecutive record, more than twice the 10-year average |
| Summer crop | 3.4 Mt; −25% y/y | Forecast 11% below the 10-year average |

These figures show why “crop production” should not be treated as one uniform product. Wheat and barley have different uses and export markets; canola is an oilseed; lentils are a pulse crop. The expected record lentil crop does not cancel out a weaker wheat outlook for a miller, just as a wheat decline does not mean every food category is becoming scarce.

## The regional split is the real agricultural story

ABARES attributes the stronger winter outlook in South Australia, Victoria and southern New South Wales to timely rainfall and good soil moisture through winter. In parts of those regions, crops matured earlier than usual and had above-average yield potential. Those conditions helped ABARES revise the national winter forecast upward by 12% compared with its June assessment.

The picture is different in northern New South Wales and much of Queensland. ABARES describes poor conditions, a significant decline in planted area and below-average yield expectations. A national average can conceal these losses because a strong result in one production region offsets weakness in another.

This regional gap matters in three ways. First, local grain receival and transport flows are not interchangeable with the national production estimate. Second, farm income is influenced by yield, quality and local costs, not just tonnes harvested. Third, the seasonal outlook for summer crops depends on soil moisture and irrigation availability in the regions where those crops are grown.

The summer crop forecast is an early warning to monitor, not a final production result. ABARES expects 3.4 million tonnes, down 25% year on year and 11% below the 10-year average. Its report points to below-average late-winter soil moisture, low irrigation-water availability and uncertain spring rainfall. Sorghum and cotton are among the crops with weaker forecasts. The next crop report will show whether planting conditions and rainfall have changed that estimate.

## Export earnings and farm margins: different from supermarket supply

Australia is a major agricultural exporter, so a smaller crop can first affect export volumes, farm revenue and commodity markets rather than immediately emptying domestic supermarket shelves. ABARES's September Agricultural Commodities Report forecasts the gross value of agricultural production to fall 5% to $99.4 billion in 2026–27 and agricultural export value to fall 5% to $76.3 billion. It also projects the average broadacre farm business profit to fall 39%, reflecting lower revenue and continuing high costs.

These are value and profitability forecasts, not a forecast of domestic food availability. The distinction is important: farmers can face a difficult margin year even when national output remains historically high. Exporters may have fewer tonnes available for overseas customers, but domestic prices also depend on crop quality, storage, contracts, transport, imports, processing capacity and the exchange rate.

For households, the link between a farm forecast and the checkout is not one-for-one. Retail prices include processing, packaging, freight, labour, energy, wholesale and retail margins. A lower wheat crop may affect flour, bread or feed-related costs, but the timing and pass-through depend on stocks and commercial contracts. It is not valid to apply the crop decline directly to the price of a loaf of bread.

## Food CPI: a useful check against the harvest headline

The ABS September 2026 CPI release recorded a 3.2% annual rise in food and non-alcoholic beverages, compared with 3.0% in August. The monthly movement for this group was 0.0% in September. Meals out and takeaway food were the main contributor to the annual increase, up 3.9%, reflecting higher operating, ingredient and other input costs.

That pattern matters because food inflation is broader than the farm harvest. It can rise because of restaurants' costs even when the measured grocery-food category is steady for the month. Conversely, a good grain crop does not guarantee that vegetables, meat, dairy or prepared meals become cheaper.

The October 2026 CPI release is scheduled for **25 November 2026**. It will help determine whether the September reading was a pause or the beginning of a different monthly trend. Readers should compare the food group with individual categories rather than relying on the all-items CPI headline.

## Fertiliser dependence is a genuine upstream exposure

The Department of Agriculture says Australia is entirely import-reliant for urea fertiliser. It also reports that 62% of Australia's fertiliser imports come from the Middle East, while much of the remaining 38% relies on Middle Eastern gas as feedstock.

That is a material supply-chain concentration. Fertiliser is needed at particular stages of the crop calendar, so the timing of a shipment can matter as much as the annual volume. A disruption that delays delivery before planting or application can be more consequential than one that occurs after the crop has passed the relevant stage.

However, import dependence is not evidence that farms have already run out of fertiliser. ABARES reported that uncertainty about fuel and fertiliser prices and availability at the start of the season did not produce significant impacts on overall production potential, although growers often paid higher prices and some prioritised applications between crops. The correct assessment is “exposure worth monitoring”, not “fertiliser shortage confirmed”.

## El Niño: use the regional forecast, not the label alone

The Bureau of Meteorology's ENSO monitoring describes a strong to very strong El Niño signal. That raises the importance of monitoring rainfall, temperature, soil moisture and water availability. It does not mean every Australian growing region will experience the same impact, nor does the climate phase alone determine yields.

For this season, the observed regional crop conditions in the ABARES report are more directly useful than a broad climate label: southern winter crops have benefited from better-than-expected rainfall, while northern New South Wales and Queensland have remained under pressure. Future effects will depend on where rain falls, when it falls, heat events, irrigation allocations and crop stage.

The practical analytical test is whether new observations change the crop estimate. A seasonal forecast should not be treated as a harvest result.

## How Australia compares with its own recent baseline

The most useful comparison in the September report is the 10-year average. Wheat at 29.9 Mt is forecast 3% above that benchmark; barley is 30% above it; canola is 37% above it; and lentils are more than twice the average. The national winter crop remains 16% above average.

This comparison is more informative than comparing only with the exceptional previous season. It prevents an unusually large harvest from becoming the misleading reference point for a “shortage” claim. It also prevents a reassuring national number from hiding crop-specific weakness: chickpea output is forecast to fall 52% to just over 1 Mt, with much of the reduction associated with northern New South Wales and Queensland.

## If you live in Australia

For local impacts, follow state agriculture department updates, the Bureau of Meteorology and the ABS price releases. A national grain forecast does not tell a household whether a particular regional product is temporarily affected. ## Assessment

Australia's 2026–27 outlook is weaker than the previous season, but the national winter crop forecast remains historically large. The risks are uneven: northern growing regions, the summer crop, farm margins and imported fertiliser exposure deserve close attention. The latest CPI reading shows food-price pressure, but it does not convert a lower crop forecast into proof of a nationwide food shortage.

The next useful update is not another generic warning. It is the December crop revision, the October CPI release and evidence about whether regional weather and input conditions have materially changed.
