> **Update — 13 November 2026:** The page now includes China's October CPI release. Summer grain output and autumn crop conditions are production indicators; the October CPI is a consumer-price indicator. Neither alone measures every part of national food security.

## Key takeaways

- China's 2026 summer grain output reached **150.746 million tonnes**, up 0.7% from 2025. Wheat output was 138.952 million tonnes, also up 0.6%.
- In October, China's CPI rose 0.7% year on year, while the food price index fell 2.9%. This was not a uniform decline in every food: eggs and beef were more expensive year on year even as fresh vegetables and pork fell.
- The key question for the rest of 2026 is whether the autumn harvest, regional weather and food-price trends remain consistent with the strong summer grain result.

## The summer grain result: higher yield offset a small area decline

China's National Bureau of Statistics reported summer grain output of 150.746 million tonnes in 2026, 1.0 million tonnes or 0.7% higher than in 2025. The result came despite a 0.2% decline in summer grain sown area. Yield per hectare increased 0.8%, to 5,681.6 kilograms.

Wheat made up the majority of the summer grain total. NBS put wheat output at 138.952 million tonnes, up 0.6%, with planted area down 0.3% and yield up 0.9%. In other words, the increase was not driven by expanding the area planted; improved average yield more than offset a modest area reduction.

| Indicator | 2026 NBS result | Year-on-year change |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total summer grain | 150.746 Mt | +0.7% |
| Wheat | 138.952 Mt | +0.6% |
| Total summer grain area | 26.532 million ha | −0.2% |
| Summer grain yield | 5,681.6 kg/ha | +0.8% |
| Wheat planted area | 23.007 million ha | −0.3% |
| Wheat yield | 6,039.6 kg/ha | +0.9% |

The figures are strong, but their scope must be understood. “Summer grain” is one part of China's annual grain harvest, not the complete annual total. Autumn grain accounts for a large share of the country's yearly output, so the later crop and the conditions in its main production regions still matter.

## Why the autumn crop is the next test

The Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs said the autumn grain planting area had risen slightly in 2026, providing a foundation for another large harvest. It also warned about more frequent extreme weather, including abrupt shifts between drought and flooding in some areas. Its report noted that the strong El Niño developing in the Pacific could bring more heat, heavier rainfall or more severe drought to particular regions.

This is a regional risk, not a prediction that all of China will experience crop failure. A climate phase can influence seasonal probabilities, but the final outcome depends on where and when rainfall occurs, irrigation availability, temperatures during sensitive crop stages, pests and the ability to respond to local warnings.

The summer grain figures are therefore a useful baseline for wheat and other early-harvested cereals. They should not be used as a substitute for monitoring autumn grain output, rice production, corn yields and regional crop conditions. The next meaningful update will be the official release of the completed autumn harvest data, not an extrapolation from the summer result.

## October food prices: deflation overall, but not across every item

China's NBS reported that the national CPI increased 0.7% year on year in October 2026. Food prices, however, decreased 2.9% year on year and 1.0% month on month. The January–October average CPI was 0.9% higher than in the same period of 2025.

The internal composition is important. Fresh vegetable prices fell 16.9% year on year, fresh fruit fell 3.8%, livestock meat fell 2.7% and pork fell 8.6%. But egg prices rose 10.1% and beef prices rose 8.7%. Grain prices were down 0.5% year on year.

This means the aggregate food index was pulled down by large declines in some items, particularly fresh vegetables, while households buying eggs or beef faced a different experience. A national average does not imply that every food became cheaper, and a decline in the food price index is not the same as a decline in the price level for every family.

The price figures also say nothing by themselves about nutritional access or household income. They measure changes in prices, not whether lower-income families can afford a balanced diet or whether remote communities have reliable access to a particular food. For that reason, the CPI should be read alongside income, employment, regional price and distribution information.

## Production and retail prices answer different questions

China's strong summer grain result and lower October food prices are broadly consistent with a food system that has had substantial supply in these segments. But the relationship is not mechanical.

The harvest data cover a defined crop category and season. The CPI covers a broad basket of food and other goods and services. Retail prices are also influenced by storage, transport, processing, distribution, imports, seasonal demand and market policy. A good wheat harvest can reduce pressure on one part of the grain balance while prices for eggs, beef or vegetables move for different reasons.

This distinction is particularly important when interpreting claims of a national shortage. A genuine supply problem would need to be identified by product, location and period. A temporary rise in one market price or a poor crop in one province cannot be generalized to the entire country. Similarly, a national harvest total cannot prove that every region is free from local disruption.

## What this means for China's food-security position

The summer result shows the value of yield gains: output rose even though planted area was slightly lower. This matters in a country where land and water resources must support a large domestic food system. It also shows why productivity, seed quality, agronomic support and reliable irrigation are as important as simply increasing planted area.

At the same time, the autumn crop is exposed to localized weather extremes. The Ministry's warning about drought-flood swings, heat and crop pests makes monitoring more useful than relying on a single national climate label. Local water availability, crop condition reports and timely weather warnings are the practical indicators of risk.

The October price data provide a second signal. Broad food prices were lower than a year earlier, but category-level changes differed significantly. A food-security assessment should therefore distinguish national availability from household affordability and local access.

## Comparison with the previous year

The direct production comparison is positive: summer grain output rose 0.7% compared with 2025, with a 0.8% increase in yield per unit area offsetting the 0.2% reduction in sown area. The NBS price comparison is mixed by product: food overall fell 2.9% year on year, but eggs and beef increased.

Taken together, the data show that production and market conditions can strengthen in aggregate while still producing uneven experiences by product and household. This is a more precise way to discuss China's food-security position than describing the country as either entirely protected or on the brink of shortage.

## What to watch next

- Official autumn grain production results, including total output, planted area and yield.
- Further NBS CPI releases, especially the separate grain, fresh vegetable, meat, egg and dairy series.
- Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs updates on drought, flood, heat and pest conditions across major autumn crop regions.
- Changes in regional wholesale and retail prices where local weather or transport conditions disrupt market supply.

## If you live in China

Use NBS price data and local agriculture, weather and market notices to distinguish a national trend from a product-specific or regional change. The October figures show why a falling aggregate food index cannot describe every shopping basket. ## Assessment

China's 2026 summer grain output was strong, supported by higher yield despite a small reduction in planted area. October food prices declined overall, although the movement differed across food categories. The next decisive evidence is the autumn harvest and subsequent regional price data. The figures reviewed here do not support a claim of a nationwide food shortage, but they do justify continued attention to regional weather and uneven consumer-price movements.
