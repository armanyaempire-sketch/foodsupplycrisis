There is no single list of countries that are “safe” or “unsafe” from food-system shocks.

Exposure depends on what a country imports, what it grows, how much it stores, how reliable its infrastructure is and whether households can afford food when prices rise.

That makes comparison more useful than a simple ranking.

## What creates food-system exposure?

Five factors appear again and again.

**Import dependence:** Countries that buy large shares of food or fertilizer from abroad are exposed to international prices and shipping.

**Route concentration:** A country that depends on one port, supplier or maritime corridor has fewer alternatives during disruption.

**Reserve capacity:** Stocks can buy time, although they cannot replace production or trade forever.

**Climate exposure:** Drought, heat and variable rainfall can affect domestic crops.

**Household purchasing power:** A country can have food available and still have severe food insecurity if households cannot afford it.

## Gulf states: strong purchasing power, high import exposure

Gulf countries have limited agricultural water resources and therefore rely heavily on imported food.

Their financial resources and logistics infrastructure provide buffers.

But a prolonged disruption to shipping, energy or fertilizer can still raise costs.

The lesson is that wealth reduces some forms of food insecurity without eliminating trade exposure.

## Egypt: grain-import concentration matters

Egypt is highly dependent on imported wheat.

That makes Black Sea shipping and international wheat prices important.

Government stocks and procurement can cushion temporary disruptions, but higher import costs can still affect public finances and bread policy.

## Brazil: a food exporter with fertilizer exposure

Brazil is a major food exporter, so it provides supply to the rest of the world.

But that does not make it immune to shocks.

Heavy fertilizer-import dependence means higher input costs can affect farm economics before the harvest.

This is a useful example of why food-export strength and input security are different measures.

## Japan: domestic rice buffers, imported inputs

Japan can use rice reserves to manage temporary supply pressure.

MAFF said in July 2026 that government rice reserves were around 320,000 tonnes against an appropriate level of about 1 million tonnes, and it later moved to buy back 210,000 tonnes of older stock.

That shows how a country can have a strong reserve policy while still paying close attention to consumer prices and future supply.

## Australia: major exporter, climate-sensitive production

ABARES' September forecast puts 2026–27 winter crop production at 61 million tonnes, down 12% but still the fourth-largest winter crop on record.

Australia therefore illustrates another form of exposure: weather can reduce output without turning the country into a national food-shortage case.

## Conflict-affected hotspots

Sudan, South Sudan, Yemen, Palestine, Northeast Nigeria and Somalia illustrate a different problem.

In these places, the key constraints can be conflict, displacement, access and livelihoods.

The food may exist somewhere, but people can still be unable to obtain it.

## Why a single score would mislead

A country can rank high on import dependence and still have strong reserves.

Another can produce most of its food but have severe household poverty.

A third can have large stocks but weak transport infrastructure.

That is why food-security comparison should use several indicators instead of one headline score.

## A practical comparison framework

When assessing a country, ask:

1. What share of food and fertilizer is imported?
2. How diversified are suppliers?
3. How large and accessible are reserves?
4. How exposed are major crops to drought, heat and rainfall changes?
5. What happens to households when prices rise?

## Bottom line

The countries most exposed to food-system shocks are not necessarily the countries with the least food.

Exposure comes from dependence and the ability to absorb disruption.

That is why food-security analysis should compare imports, reserves, climate, logistics and purchasing power together.
