The FAO Food Price Index is one of the most widely followed measures of international food commodity prices. It is useful because it provides a consistent monthly snapshot of the direction of globally traded food commodities.

It is also easy to misunderstand.

A rising FAO index does not mean every supermarket is raising prices by the same percentage. It does not mean every country has a food shortage. And it does not measure the cost of one household's complete grocery basket.

## What is the FAO Food Price Index?

FAO's index tracks international prices for a basket of major food commodity groups. The series covers cereals, vegetable oils, dairy, meat and sugar.

That makes it a market indicator rather than a direct household inflation measure.

A country can experience faster food inflation than the international index if its currency weakens or its domestic crops perform poorly. Another country can see a smaller increase because it has strong domestic production or uses price controls and subsidies.

## What happened in September 2026?

FAO reported that the index averaged 136.0 points in September 2026.

That was 1.5% higher than the revised August level and 5.8% above September 2025.

Cereal, vegetable-oil and sugar prices increased. Meat prices declined and dairy prices were broadly stable.

FAO linked the overall increase mainly to transportation disruptions, weather concerns and higher quotations in crop-based commodity groups.

## Why did cereal prices rise?

Cereals are particularly important because wheat, maize and rice feed both households and livestock systems.

In September, FAO reported stronger cereal quotations as trade disruptions and weather conditions affected market expectations and availability.

The Black Sea is an important part of the wheat and maize trade network. When shipments are delayed or become more expensive, buyers may compete for supplies from other exporters.

That can lift international prices even if global production remains large.

## Why does sugar matter?

Sugar prices can move for reasons that have little to do with wheat or rice.

Crop conditions in major producing countries, harvest timing, export availability and weather can all change the sugar market.

This is why the sub-indices are useful. The headline number tells you that the overall basket moved; the components help explain why.

## Why the FAO index is not your grocery bill

The FAO index measures internationally traded commodities.

Your local grocery bill also reflects:

- domestic production;
- wages;
- fuel and electricity;
- transport and storage;
- exchange rates;
- taxes;
- retail margins;
- seasonal conditions;
- and what foods households actually purchase.

A global commodity can rise 5% without producing a 5% increase in a household's food spending.

## How to read the index intelligently

Three questions are more useful than simply asking whether the index went up.

First, which commodity groups moved?

Second, what caused the move: production, weather, trade, energy or demand?

Third, is the change temporary or persistent?

A one-month jump can be important, but a prolonged sequence of increases is a different signal.

## Why the index matters for food security

International prices matter most when they interact with weak purchasing power.

An importing country can face higher food costs when commodity prices rise, its currency falls and freight costs increase at the same time.

Low-income households have less room to absorb those changes.

That is why international price indicators should be read together with domestic food inflation and country-level food-security assessments.

## What to watch next

For future updates, track the FAO index alongside cereal production forecasts, fertilizer prices, energy markets, freight costs and regional food-price data.

The index is most useful as part of a dashboard rather than as a standalone crisis signal.

## What an index point means—and what it does not mean

FAO’s Food Price Index is a measure of monthly changes in international prices for a basket of globally traded food commodities. It combines five commodity-group indices—cereals, vegetable oils, dairy, meat and sugar—weighted by average export shares over 2014–2016. The underlying methodology and downloads are available from the [official FAO index page](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/foodpricesindex/en/).

An index-point level is not a price per kilogram, a supermarket bill or a measure of how much every item costs. The percentage change over a month or year is often more interpretable, and the sub-indices show whether movements are concentrated in particular commodity groups. A national consumer-price index answers another question: it measures prices paid in that country and may include local products, distribution costs, taxes and other factors not directly reflected in international export quotations.

FAO’s release dated 2 October 2026 said the index averaged 136.0 points in September, up 1.5% from its revised August level and 5.8% from a year earlier. It also noted that the index remained 15.1% below its March 2022 peak. That context matters: a recent rise can be real and worth tracking without meaning that the series is at an all-time high. The same release reported higher cereal, vegetable-oil and sugar indices while the meat index declined and dairy was broadly stable.

## A repeatable way to read each monthly release

First, record the release date and the latest month covered. Then note the headline index and both monthly and annual changes. Next, inspect each commodity-group index to see whether the move is broad or concentrated. Finally, read the explanatory notes about the drivers—such as harvest expectations, export availability, weather or transport—and compare those notes with the cereal supply outlook and relevant national data.

Do not use the FAO index alone to forecast the size or date of a retail price change. Exchange rates, local harvests, taxes, subsidies, storage, energy and retail contracts can delay or amplify pass-through. And do not use price indices alone to measure hunger: for that, the relevant evidence concerns access to food, household resources and current humanitarian assessments.

This method allows readers to distinguish a monthly market signal from a sustained trend and keeps the interpretation tied to what the index actually measures.

## Bottom line

The September 2026 FAO Food Price Index confirms that international food commodity markets are under renewed pressure.

It does not prove a universal food shortage.

The important information is inside the headline number: which foods are moving, what is driving them and whether the pressure is reaching households in particular countries.
