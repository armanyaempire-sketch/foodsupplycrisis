Fertilizer security is increasingly being treated as part of food security.

That does not necessarily mean every country needs to make every nutrient domestically.

The stronger goal is to reduce dependence on a single supplier, route or production method while using nutrients more efficiently.

## Build some domestic capacity

The United States is one example.

USDA launched the FIELDS program in 2026 with up to $500 million available to support domestic fertilizer production capacity and supply-chain resilience.

The policy does not eliminate imports. It adds another source of supply.

## Diversify imports

Import diversification can be just as important as domestic production.

A country that buys fertilizer from several origins is less exposed to a disruption at one port or producer.

But diversification can increase procurement costs, especially if the alternative supply is farther away.

## Improve nutrient efficiency

Efficiency can reduce exposure without reducing productivity.

Soil testing, precision application, better timing and balanced nutrient use can prevent unnecessary application.

The right amount depends on soil, crop and local conditions.

## Rebuild soil health

Healthy soils can improve nutrient retention and water management.

Organic matter, crop rotation, cover crops and other practices can contribute to resilience where they fit the farming system.

These are long-term measures, not emergency fixes.

## Protect farmers from extreme volatility

A fertilizer shortage is hardest for farmers when prices jump suddenly and credit is limited.

Clear market information, appropriate financing and predictable policy can help producers plan purchases.

## Invest in storage and logistics

Supply security includes the ability to move fertilizer when it arrives.

Ports, railways, warehouses and domestic transport all matter.

A country can have enough fertilizer on paper and still experience a shortage in a farming region if deliveries arrive too late.

## What about fertilizer subsidies?

Subsidies can protect farmers during shocks, but they also have costs.

The long-term question is whether policy improves resilience, efficiency and competition rather than permanently hiding an underlying supply problem.

## Resilient farming is broader than fertilizer

Fertilizer is one input.

Water, seed quality, weather, pest management, market access and crop choice also determine yields.

A resilient farming strategy therefore combines input security with good agronomy.

## Resilience is not the same as producing every input domestically

“Fertilizer independence” can mean several different things: producing more fertilizer at home, diversifying import suppliers, making distribution more reliable, or reducing the amount of nutrients needed for a given crop outcome. Those strategies have different costs and time horizons. A domestic factory may reduce one kind of exposure but still depend on imported gas, phosphate rock, potash, spare parts or shipping.

| Measure | What it can improve | Constraint to plan for |
|---|---|---|
| Diversify suppliers and routes | Lowers reliance on one exporter or corridor | Alternative supply may cost more and still share common risks |
| Use nutrients more efficiently | Reduces avoidable input use and runoff | Recommendations need to fit soil tests, crop and local conditions |
| Maintain suitable stocks | Helps bridge short delivery delays | Storage, financing and the risk of holding the wrong product matter |
| Improve soil health and nutrient cycling | Can support long-term efficiency | Benefits differ by soil and cannot always replace required nutrients immediately |
| Support farm advice and monitoring | Helps farmers use inputs at the right rate and time | Advice must be practical, accessible and adapted to local crops |

A resilience plan should be evaluated against actual outcomes: availability at planting time, farm costs, crop yields, soil condition and environmental effects. Reducing fertilizer use indiscriminately can also reduce yield, so “less input” should not automatically be treated as “more resilient.” The goal is to use appropriate nutrients reliably and efficiently.

To judge current market exposure, compare official national input data with the [FAO Cereal Supply and Demand Brief](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/csdb) and the [AMIS Market Monitor](https://www.amis-outlook.org/). Keep price changes separate from physical availability and note when the crop must receive the input. If a supply problem occurs after the relevant application window, a later price decline may not reverse its effect on that season’s production.


## Measure fertilizer resilience by farm outcomes

A national fertilizer strategy should be evaluated at the point where inputs are used: can farmers obtain the right nutrient, in the right quantity and before the crop’s application window? Counting domestic production alone can overstate resilience if factories depend on imported gas or raw materials, if distribution is uneven, or if the product is unavailable in the region where it is needed.

A useful dashboard combines the price and availability of each nutrient with delivery timing, import concentration, farm application and crop outcomes. It should also track soil testing and environmental impacts. Applying less fertilizer may lower cost and runoff where past use was excessive, but indiscriminate reductions can reduce yield. The purpose of nutrient efficiency is to match application to crop need, not to replace technical advice with a blanket “use less” rule.

For public programmes, three distinctions are important. **Capacity** measures whether production or import options exist. **Access** measures whether suppliers and farmers can actually obtain and distribute the product. **Effectiveness** measures whether timely use supports crop productivity and farm viability. An effective strategy publishes information on all three, including the price farmers pay and the constraints that remain.

The [AMIS Market Monitor](https://www.amis-outlook.org/) and FAO’s [cereal market outlook](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/csdb) can help establish international context. Pair these with dated national fertilizer and agriculture releases. A market report should not imply a yield loss until crop or farm evidence supports it, and should not call a country independent when essential feedstocks still depend on external supply. A realistic strategy reduces concentrated risks while preserving access to the inputs needed for productive farming.

## Bottom line

Fertilizer independence should not be interpreted as isolation.

The more practical goal is a diversified system in which domestic capacity, multiple suppliers, efficient nutrient use, healthy soils and reliable logistics work together.

That reduces the chance that one fertilizer disruption becomes a larger food-supply problem.
