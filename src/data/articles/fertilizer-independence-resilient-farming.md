Fertilizer security is increasingly being treated as part of food security.

That does not necessarily mean every country needs to make every nutrient domestically.

The stronger goal is to reduce dependence on a single supplier, route or production method while using nutrients more efficiently.

## Build some domestic capacity

The United States is one example.

USDA launched the FIELDS program in 2026 with up to $500 million available to support domestic fertilizer production capacity and supply-chain resilience.

The policy does not eliminate imports. It adds another source of supply.

## Diversify imports

Import diversification can be just as important as domestic production.

A country that buys fertilizer from several origins is less exposed to a disruption at one port or producer.

But diversification can increase procurement costs, especially if the alternative supply is farther away.

## Improve nutrient efficiency

Efficiency can reduce exposure without reducing productivity.

Soil testing, precision application, better timing and balanced nutrient use can prevent unnecessary application.

The right amount depends on soil, crop and local conditions.

## Rebuild soil health

Healthy soils can improve nutrient retention and water management.

Organic matter, crop rotation, cover crops and other practices can contribute to resilience where they fit the farming system.

These are long-term measures, not emergency fixes.

## Protect farmers from extreme volatility

A fertilizer shortage is hardest for farmers when prices jump suddenly and credit is limited.

Clear market information, appropriate financing and predictable policy can help producers plan purchases.

## Invest in storage and logistics

Supply security includes the ability to move fertilizer when it arrives.

Ports, railways, warehouses and domestic transport all matter.

A country can have enough fertilizer on paper and still experience a shortage in a farming region if deliveries arrive too late.

## What about fertilizer subsidies?

Subsidies can protect farmers during shocks, but they also have costs.

The long-term question is whether policy improves resilience, efficiency and competition rather than permanently hiding an underlying supply problem.

## Resilient farming is broader than fertilizer

Fertilizer is one input.

Water, seed quality, weather, pest management, market access and crop choice also determine yields.

A resilient farming strategy therefore combines input security with good agronomy.



## Bottom line

Fertilizer independence should not be interpreted as isolation.

The more practical goal is a diversified system in which domestic capacity, multiple suppliers, efficient nutrient use, healthy soils and reliable logistics work together.

That reduces the chance that one fertilizer disruption becomes a larger food-supply problem.

## Resilience is not the same as fertilizer autarky

Reducing exposure to fertilizer shocks does not require every country to manufacture every nutrient domestically. A resilient system combines several tools: diversified import origins, reliable transport and storage, efficient use of nutrients, soil testing, recycling of suitable organic materials and domestic production where it is economically and environmentally viable.

These options solve different problems. A local ammonia or urea plant may reduce exposure to one imported product, but it can still depend on natural gas, electricity, spare parts and finance. Better nutrient efficiency can reduce the amount needed per unit of output, but it cannot replace a missing nutrient where soils are deficient. Organic recycling can return nutrients to fields, although its supply, composition and transport costs limit how much it can replace commercial fertilizer.

A serious resilience plan should therefore measure more than domestic factory capacity. Track the concentration of import suppliers, the reliability of energy inputs, fertilizer prices relative to farm-gate crop prices, soil-test coverage, application efficiency and the condition of distribution networks. The goal is dependable access at a cost farmers can afford—not self-sufficiency measured by one production figure.

## Measure resilience by farmer access, not factory capacity alone

A fertilizer strategy should be evaluated at the point where nutrients reach farms. Announced plant capacity is not the same as reliable commercial output, and output is not the same as affordable product available before the local application window. A plant may depend on imported gas, electricity, catalysts, spare parts, port access and working capital. A domestic production target can therefore overstate resilience if the plant's critical inputs remain concentrated in one supply route.

A better dashboard tracks the share of imports sourced from the largest suppliers, delivery time to major farming regions, retail price relative to crop revenue, credit access, and whether dealers have the required nutrient grade in the planting period. Soil testing and extension services matter too: they help farmers apply the right amount rather than respond to high prices by cutting nutrients indiscriminately. The strongest policy mix diversifies exposure and improves nutrient-use efficiency while keeping environmental losses and farmers' cash constraints visible.

## Choose the resilience model that fits the country's resource base

There is no single fertilizer-independence model. The economically sensible mix depends on energy availability, mineral resources, farm structure, import concentration and the nutrient needs of local crops.

| Country or system type | Long-term policy priority | What to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Gas-producing country with reliable low-cost energy | Assess whether domestic ammonia or urea production is competitive and resilient to plant outages | Plant utilisation, gas opportunity cost, maintenance, emissions and delivered farm-gate price. |
| Import-dependent country without cheap gas | Diversify suppliers and ports, improve purchasing coordination and maintain regional distribution stocks | Supplier concentration, shipping lead time, currency exposure and delivery before the crop's application window. |
| Smallholder-dominated farming system | Improve soil testing, extension, cooperative purchasing and access to the correct nutrient grade | Nutrient-use efficiency, dealer coverage, credit cost and yield response on representative soils. |
| Intensive irrigated system | Pair nutrient efficiency with water management and crop-specific application timing | Soil organic matter, nutrient losses, irrigation quality and water productivity. |

Domestic factory capacity should not be treated as a success measure by itself. A plant that depends on one imported energy source or cannot deliver affordable product to farms may add capacity without making farmers more secure.
