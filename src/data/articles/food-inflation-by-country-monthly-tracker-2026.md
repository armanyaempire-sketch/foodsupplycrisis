> **Update — 19 November 2026:** Added the latest October 2026 official releases available for the United States, United Kingdom and euro area, alongside the existing global, India and Australia series. The measures are deliberately labelled rather than ranked as if they were identical.

Food inflation is one of the easiest food-system indicators to understand and one of the easiest to misuse.

A global commodity index tells you about international markets.

A national food-inflation measure tells you what happened to a basket of food and beverages in a particular economy.

Those numbers answer different questions.

This page is designed as a recurring tracker.

## How to read the table

Use the latest official national release available.

Always record:

- country;
- reference month;
- annual food-inflation rate;
- monthly change where available;
- measurement definition;
- and release date.

Older numbers should stay visible only when they provide historical context.

| Euro area | October 2026 | HICP, food, alcohol and tobacco | +1.5% year on year |

The global figure is an international commodity index, not consumer inflation. FAO reported that October's index was also 16.4% below its March 2022 peak.

India's October CFPI figure is provisional. The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation reported 8.94% year-on-year food inflation, with 9.09% in rural areas and 8.68% in urban areas. September's corresponding figure was final at 7.74%.

Australia's September 2026 figure is the annual movement for the Food and non-alcoholic beverages group in the weighted average of eight capital cities. The ABS reported a 0.0% monthly change for that group in September, so the 3.2% figure should not be mistaken for the monthly movement.

The United States row uses the BLS **food-at-home** component, not the complete food index: the October release reported +0.3% over 12 months and a +0.4% seasonally adjusted change from September. The complete food index rose 0.4% over 12 months and 0.5% month on month. The UK row is the ONS food-only CPI component; it rose 0.8% over the year and 0.2% during October. Eurostat's 1.5% figure is the broader **food, alcohol and tobacco** HICP aggregate for the euro area, not a food-only national CPI.

The release dates are also different. BLS published its October report on 10 November 2026; ONS and Eurostat published their October figures on 18 November. A tracker should use the latest release available on its update date rather than imply that every country publishes on the same schedule.

## Why countries cannot be compared blindly

Definitions differ.

One country may publish a food-only consumer index.

Another may use a broader food-and-beverages category.

Weights also differ because households in different countries spend different amounts on different foods.

That means a 5% food-inflation reading in one country is not necessarily equivalent to 5% in another.

## What the global FAO index adds

FAO's Food Price Index averaged 133.9 points in October 2026, down 1.2% from its revised September level but 5.9% above October 2025. Cereals, dairy and meat eased; sugar rose 6.6% month on month and vegetable oils increased marginally.

This provides the international backdrop.

## Why domestic inflation can move differently

A country can see food inflation rise while international commodity prices are stable.

Domestic harvests, exchange rates, electricity, transport, taxes and retail margins all matter.

The reverse can also happen: global commodity prices may rise while domestic prices remain relatively contained for a period because of strong local production or government policy.

## How to update this tracker

Each month, replace each country's “latest verified reading” with the newest official release.

Keep the previous month in a historical table rather than overwriting the record completely.

Link to the national statistical agency.

Record whether the number is provisional or final.

If a source revises an earlier figure, note the revision date.

## What the tracker cannot tell you

Food inflation alone does not tell you whether a country has a physical shortage.

A country can have high inflation with adequate food availability.

Another can have relatively low inflation while a conflict-affected population faces severe food insecurity.

That is why the tracker should be read alongside [Food Security](/food-security/) and [Global Food Crisis 2026](/articles/global-food-crisis-2026/).



## Bottom line

Food inflation is a recurring signal worth watching because it shows what households are facing.

But the most useful tracker is built from comparable definitions, official releases and clear dates—not from a collection of viral price claims.

This page should be updated monthly as new national statistics are published.

## How to compare country food-inflation figures fairly

A country tracker is useful only when the entries measure similar things. One statistic may be the annual change in a food consumer-price index; another may be a monthly movement; a third may cover food and non-alcoholic beverages or only selected staples. Those values should not be ranked as though they were one common measure.

For every entry, check the statistical agency, release date, reference month, comparison period, geographic coverage and revision status. Keep **month-on-month** and **year-on-year** rates in separate columns. Mark provisional data clearly and replace them only when the agency publishes a revised figure. Where a national series separates rural and urban prices, retain that distinction instead of averaging away a difference that matters to households.

Inflation also does not tell the whole food-security story. A high rate can occur while food remains available; a low rate can coexist with poor access for households whose incomes have fallen. For a fuller reading, pair the price series with wage or income context, staple-market prices and, where relevant, public food assistance or humanitarian access. The tracker is a comparison tool, not a standalone shortage index.
