No reliable source can publish a guaranteed list of countries that will experience food shortages in 2027. The useful answer is an evidence-led food shortage 2027 risk list: the conditions that could tighten supply, raise prices or make food harder to access—and the data that would confirm each risk. A risk is not a prediction, and a price increase is not automatically a physical shortage.

## Latest baseline: October 2026 data released on 6 November

FAO’s 6 November 2026 update puts world cereal production at 2,999 million tonnes, the second-highest level on record. Cereal stocks are forecast to rise 1.8%, with a 32.4% stocks-to-use ratio, while 2026/27 cereal trade is forecast to contract 3.3% to 507.7 million tonnes. The October FAO Food Price Index averaged 133.9 points, down 1.2% from September but 5.9% above October 2025. This is a mixed baseline: large aggregate production and rising stocks alongside tighter trade and different movements among commodities.

FAO’s November 2026 list identifies 41 countries or territories currently requiring external food assistance. That is a present assessment of severe food needs, not a forecast that all 41 will face a national physical shortage in 2027. Check the [FAO country list and its stated reasons](https://www.fao.org/giews/country-analysis/external-assistance/en/) before treating any country list as a prediction.

## Food shortage 2027 list: seven risks to monitor

| Risk | What would confirm it | What would weaken the concern |
|---|---|---|
| Weather affecting several major crop regions | Updated national and international crop forecasts show simultaneous deterioration in important producing areas | Crop conditions recover or other exporters have enough supply to offset losses |
| Fertilizer availability before planting | Deliveries or affordability worsen during the actual purchase and application window | Farmers can obtain the needed nutrients on time, even if prices remain above older averages |
| Shipping and port disruption | Vessel flows, export loadings or port access remain constrained long enough to delay supply | Routes reopen, freight pressure eases and importers can use alternative routes |
| Export restrictions or sudden policy changes | Major suppliers limit exports or change rules in ways that reduce internationally available supply | Export policies remain predictable and alternative origins can fill gaps |
| Falling usable stocks or exportable surplus | Stock-to-use measures, inventory quality or exporter availability deteriorate across more than one supplier | Stocks remain adequate and exportable supply is available when buyers need it |
| Import financing and currency pressure | Importers cannot secure foreign exchange, credit or insurance to purchase available food | Financing improves and importers can pay for and move contracted supplies |
| Conflict and blocked market access | Displacement, damaged infrastructure or insecurity prevent people from reaching food and markets | Safe access, local trade and humanitarian delivery improve in affected areas |

The risks matter most when they overlap. A poor crop in one region may be offset by another exporter. A port delay may be manageable when stocks and credit are available. The combination of production losses, input constraints, trade disruption and reduced household purchasing power is more serious than any single signal.

## Is there an official list of countries expected to face food shortages in 2027?

There is no single authoritative list that can reliably name every country that will experience a food shortage in 2027. Forecasts have different publication dates and measure different things. FAO’s November 2026 list of countries requiring external food assistance is a current warning and monitoring resource; it must not be relabelled as a guaranteed 2027 shortage list. For country-level outlooks, read the latest [FAO GIEWS country assessments](https://www.fao.org/giews/country-analysis/external-assistance/en/) and check the assessment period, affected population and stated driver.

## How to read the scenarios below

The four scenarios that follow describe possible paths for food prices and supply pressure. They are conditional pathways, not probability estimates and not a claim that one outcome is certain.

## Scenario 1: Trade normalizes

In a more favorable scenario, shipping conditions improve, trade routes operate more normally and fertilizer availability stabilizes.

Freight and insurance costs could fall.

Importers would have more options.

That would reduce one source of upward pressure.

## Scenario 2: Weather remains disruptive

A strong El Niño can create very different outcomes across producing regions.

If important crops suffer in several major exporters, commodity prices could remain under pressure.

The crucial issue would be whether alternative suppliers can compensate.

## Scenario 3: Input costs stay high

Fertilizer and energy costs can affect farmers before the next harvest.

If farmers face persistently high input costs, production decisions may change.

That does not guarantee lower production, but it can keep the cost structure elevated.

## Scenario 4: Multiple shocks overlap

The most difficult scenario would involve poor weather, shipping disruption, high fertilizer costs and weak purchasing power at the same time.

That combination could create a stronger price shock than any one factor alone.

## What could bring prices down?

Prices do not need a perfect food system to decline.

A stronger harvest can help.

Shipping conditions can improve.

Energy costs can fall.

Importers can find alternative suppliers.

And high prices themselves can reduce demand for some commodities.

## Why forecasts should be treated carefully

Forecasts are conditional.

An agency or analyst may assume a particular weather pattern, harvest size or trade route.

When those assumptions change, the forecast changes.

That is why a 2027 food-price page should be updated as new data arrives.

## What to monitor

Track the FAO Food Price Index, major crop forecasts, fertilizer prices, energy, freight, Black Sea shipping and El Niño indicators.

For household budgeting, also watch domestic food inflation.

Global prices are a signal, not a direct grocery-price forecast.

## Treat these as scenarios with observable triggers

The scenarios above are not probabilities and should not be read as a prediction that one outcome is certain. Their purpose is to show how the same starting conditions can develop differently depending on trade, weather, input costs and policy. Each scenario should be updated when a source changes—not simply when a new month arrives.

| Scenario | Signals that would support it | Evidence that would weaken it |
|---|---|---|
| Trade normalizes | More reliable shipment flows, less freight pressure and improved export availability | Persistent route disruption or repeated export revisions |
| Weather pressure persists | Updated crop forecasts deteriorate in important production regions | Improved rainfall and crop-condition reports |
| Input costs stay high | Fuel and fertilizer remain expensive and deliveries stay constrained | Lower prices plus reliable supply before the crop application window |
| Several shocks overlap | Multiple major exporters face constraints while trade and stocks tighten | Strong harvests, available substitutes and improving trade flows |

A responsible forecast should also distinguish the direction of prices from the size of a move. The FAO Food Price Index provides a global commodity signal; it does not directly forecast the food CPI of every country. Domestic outcomes depend on exchange rates, local harvests, taxes, subsidies, retail competition and the share of imported food and inputs.

For the baseline, use the [FAO Food Price Index](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/foodpricesindex/en/) and [Cereal Supply and Demand Brief](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/csdb), and state the date of the release being used. Compare updated forecasts with their previous versions so readers can see what has changed. A revised estimate is often more informative than a dramatic headline because it reveals whether the underlying supply balance is improving or deteriorating.

If the data points in different directions, report a mixed outlook. For example, prices may rise while production remains historically high, or a market may ease while food insecurity persists in a conflict-affected region. Those are not contradictions if the indicators measure different parts of the food system.


## A monthly update template for the 2027 outlook

For each update, record the source release date, the newest period covered, the previous estimate and the revised estimate. Then explain what changed: crop expectations, shipment volumes, fertilizer availability, energy costs, currency movements or domestic prices. This makes the page a monitored scenario analysis instead of an evergreen page that repeats the same forecast every month.

A compact monthly record can use five indicators:

| Indicator | Track consistently | Why |
|---|---|---|
| FAO Food Price Index | Monthly level and percentage changes | Shows international commodity-price direction |
| Cereal outlook | Output, trade and stocks revisions | Helps distinguish high prices from a worsening global balance |
| Fertilizer and energy | Price and availability evidence | Signals pressure on future crop costs |
| Shipping and trade | Routes, freight and exporter availability | Tests whether logistics risk is easing or spreading |
| Domestic food inflation | Official local consumer-price series | Shows whether global pressure is reaching household budgets |

The report should also say what changed in the interpretation. If global commodity prices rise while production forecasts remain historically strong, explain the market and distribution mechanisms instead of calling it a global production collapse. If prices fall but a conflict-affected population still cannot access food, report that humanitarian conditions require a different measure.

Use the newest releases from the [FAO Food Price Index](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/foodpricesindex/en/) and [Cereal Supply and Demand Brief](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/csdb), plus national statistical data for local outcomes. Do not fill a gap with an invented figure when a source has not yet published the period. Label the latest available data with its actual month and update the scenario when a source provides new evidence.

## Use observable triggers instead of assigning false precision to a forecast

A scenario is useful only when it specifies what would have to happen. In a normalization scenario, freight and fertilizer availability improve, major crop forecasts remain stable and export restrictions do not expand. In a weather-disruption scenario, crop-condition reports deteriorate across more than one major producing region. In an input-cost scenario, fertilizer and energy costs remain high through planting periods. A compound-shock scenario requires several of those pressures to overlap.

Do not assign a probability to these paths unless a transparent forecasting model supports it. Instead, keep a dated log of the signals: FAO commodity indices, national crop forecasts, fertilizer prices, freight rates, export policy and local consumer inflation. Each month, record what changed, which scenario gained or lost support, and what evidence is still missing. This makes the outlook auditable and prevents a single dramatic headline from becoming a supposedly certain prediction.

## Bottom line

Food prices could rise in 2027, stay elevated or moderate.

The outcome will depend on harvests, shipping, fertilizer, energy and household purchasing power.

The most useful forecast is therefore not a single number but a transparent set of conditions that can be monitored as evidence changes.
