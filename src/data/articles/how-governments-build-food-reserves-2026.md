> **Update — 19 November 2026:** Added storage-capacity rules by commodity and climate. Public reserves are now evaluated by warehouse condition, moisture control, cold-chain reliability and distribution reach, not just announced tonnage.

Food reserves are one of the oldest tools governments use to manage supply risk.

But a reserve is not a magic number.

A country can have large grain stocks and still experience high food inflation. Another can maintain smaller public reserves but rely on diversified imports and strong commercial storage.

What matters is how the entire system works.

## Why governments hold food reserves

Reserves can bridge short disruptions.

They can also support public distribution, stabilize markets after harvest failures and give governments time to secure imports.

The most effective systems usually define what is stored, who controls it, when it can be released and how it will be replenished.

## China: large-scale grain management

China combines large domestic agricultural production with extensive grain storage and market-management capacity.

Its 2026 summer grain harvest reached 150.746 million tonnes, according to the National Bureau of Statistics.

That production provides an important domestic buffer.

But grain reserves still need to be stored, rotated and distributed.

## Switzerland: compulsory and household preparedness

Switzerland uses a different model.

Its National Economic Supply system maintains compulsory stocks of essential goods, while the government also recommends households keep a small emergency reserve.

The federal guidance explicitly says household supplies are intended to bridge temporary bottlenecks rather than prolonged crises.

## Sweden and other European systems

European countries use a range of preparedness approaches.

Some emphasize diversified imports and private inventories.

Others have expanded national emergency planning or strategic reserves for selected products.

The exact model depends on geography, trade exposure and domestic production.

## Gulf states: reserves plus imports

Gulf countries face a particular challenge because water constraints limit domestic agricultural production.

They therefore use a combination of imported food, storage facilities, commercial inventories and alternative logistics.

The objective is to keep supplies flowing when a shipping route is disrupted.

## What makes a reserve useful?

Five things matter:

**Quantity:** enough to bridge a realistic disruption.

**Location:** stocks need to be accessible where they are needed.

**Quality:** food must remain safe and usable.

**Rotation:** old stock has to be replaced.

**Distribution:** governments need a practical system for releasing supplies.

A huge stock that cannot be moved is less useful than a smaller stock with reliable logistics.

## Why reserves do not guarantee low prices

A government may release grain to protect availability without completely reversing market prices.

Transport, wages, energy, retail and other costs remain.

This explains why countries can maintain large reserves while households still face expensive groceries.



## Bottom line

A public reserve is credible only when its physical quantity, quality, ownership, custodian and release rules can be independently understood. Announced targets and purchase contracts matter, but they should never be counted as grain already stored and ready for distribution.

## What a public food reserve can—and cannot—do

A public reserve is a policy instrument, not simply a warehouse full of food. Governments may hold stocks to respond to an emergency, smooth a temporary market disruption, support vulnerable consumers or maintain supplies of a strategically important staple. Those purposes require different release rules. A reserve used for emergency distribution is not necessarily designed to keep retail prices low every month.

A headline tonnage is incomplete without the stock's condition and accessibility. Ask whether the figure describes physical stocks already in storage, purchases that have been contracted, a planned procurement target or an estimate of commercial inventories. Then check the commodity, location, age and quality of the stock, the rate at which it can be released, and which households or buyers are eligible to receive it.

Stock rotation and transparency are central to effectiveness. A reserve that is not inspected, replenished or released according to clear rules can lose value through spoilage, storage costs or delayed decisions. The best comparison between countries is therefore not just tonnes held; it is the purpose, governance, quality, release mechanism and evidence that the reserve can reach the intended users.

## How to judge whether a reserve is operational

An operational reserve needs a public purpose, an accountable custodian and a release mechanism that can be used in time. A useful public report identifies the commodity, quality grade, storage location, physical quantity, inspection date and whether the stock is owned by the state or held under contract. It should also explain the conditions under which releases can occur and which buyers or households can receive the product.

Procurement and release decisions create trade-offs. Buying too aggressively can raise local prices or crowd out private traders; releasing too slowly can make a reserve irrelevant during a short disruption. Rotation reduces spoilage but means the headline inventory changes as older grain is sold and replaced. Compare stocks with domestic consumption or expected use only when both figures use compatible units and periods. A credible reserve policy is transparent about these limits and publishes deliveries and releases separately from targets, tenders and announcements.

## Match reserve infrastructure to the commodity and climate

A reserve is only usable while the stored food remains safe and can be moved to buyers. Infrastructure that works for dry grain may be unsuitable for chilled foods or humid coastal conditions.

| Reserve type | Storage capacity that actually matters | Operational checks |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk wheat, maize and other dry grain | Silo volume plus drying and aeration throughput; safe moisture depends on grain type, temperature, storage duration and the facility | Measure intake moisture by lot, monitor grain temperature and pests, maintain aeration, inspect roofs and drainage, and keep a stock ledger by grade and location. |
| Bagged rice or pulses in humid regions | Dry raised floor space, pallets, roof protection, ventilation that matches local humidity and pest barriers | Keep bags off the floor and away from damp walls; inspect for insects and mould; separate wet lots and rotate stock before quality deteriorates. |
| Meat, dairy, fish and other chilled products | Refrigerated space plus reliable electricity, backup power, temperature monitoring and spare parts | Track temperature continuously, validate backup power under load, and set a plan for stock transfer or safe disposal during a prolonged outage. |
| Emergency food held near conflict or flood-prone areas | Distributed, accessible storage rather than one large central warehouse | Check road access, flood height, security, staff availability and the time required to move stock to distribution points. |

A reserve manager should never count the nominal building capacity as usable reserve capacity. Usable tonnes are limited by the weakest link: safe storage, quality control, trained staff, power, handling equipment, transport and the ability to release stock before it spoils. The FAO's warehouse and bulk-storage guidance highlights moisture, aeration, pest protection and site drainage as core controls; local design must account for the actual climate and commodity.
