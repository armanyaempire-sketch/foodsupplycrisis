Water planning is different from food planning because water is both essential and heavy.

A short interruption can become difficult very quickly if a household has no safe drinking supply.

The safest plan is simple: use suitable containers, protect the water, label it and rotate it according to reliable guidance.

## How much water should a household store?

There is no single global number because official recommendations differ by country and situation.

Switzerland's federal emergency guidance recommends at least 9 litres per person and says that amount is intended to cover drinking and cooking for three days.

Other authorities use different assumptions.

Use the recommendation provided by your national or local emergency agency when one is available.

## Choose the right container

Use food-grade containers designed to hold drinking water.

Avoid containers that previously held chemicals, fuel or other hazardous materials.

Commercially bottled water is another practical option, particularly for households that do not have suitable reusable containers.

## Store water in the right place

Keep stored water somewhere cool and away from direct sunlight and chemicals.

Protect containers from damage.

Do not place drinking-water containers beside cleaning products, fuels or pesticides where leaks could contaminate them.

## Label and rotate

Write the date on containers that you fill yourself.

Rotate stored water according to the instructions provided by your public-health authority or the container manufacturer.

Use older supplies as part of normal household consumption and replace them.

## What about purification?

Purification requirements depend on the source.

If authorities issue a boil-water notice, water-treatment instructions or a do-not-drink order, follow those instructions.

For untreated water, use a method recommended by a public-health authority for the specific situation.

Do not assume that every household filter removes every contaminant.

## Remember water for cooking

Emergency water is not only for drinking.

Dry foods such as rice and pasta may require water for preparation.

This is why an emergency pantry and water supply should be planned together.

## What if the power goes out?

A power outage can affect water pumping, refrigeration and water-treatment infrastructure.

Keep some water that can be consumed without cooking.

Also consider how you would obtain additional safe water if the interruption lasts longer than expected.

## How households can prepare without overbuying

Build the reserve gradually.

Keep the supply in an accessible place.

Check it as part of normal household maintenance.

Replace damaged containers or expired supplies.

The goal is a dependable buffer, not a basement full of water that nobody can safely manage.



## Bottom line

A resilient response is simple: verify the risk, inventory what you already have, secure safe water, maintain several days of familiar shelf-stable food, protect refrigeration, rotate supplies and follow official guidance. Do not turn uncertainty into panic buying. The goal is to make a real disruption manageable while avoiding waste, financial strain and unnecessary pressure on the local food system.

## The safety test is the source, container and storage history

Water storage is not only a question of quantity. First identify whether the water is commercially sealed, supplied as potable tap water or collected from another source. The treatment and storage instructions depend on that starting point. Never assume that clear-looking water is safe, and do not treat water contaminated by fuel or toxic chemicals as though boiling or routine disinfection will make it potable.

For self-filled supplies, use a clean, food-grade container intended for drinking water. A container that previously held chemicals, fuel or cleaning products is unsuitable. Keep the container closed, clearly labelled and protected from direct sunlight, heat and physical damage. Follow current public-health guidance for cleaning, treatment and replacement intervals, because those instructions can differ by container and source.

During an actual water advisory, follow the local water authority or public-health agency rather than relying on a general household checklist. Boil-water notices, do-not-drink notices and chemical contamination warnings are not interchangeable. The notice tells you which uses are unsafe and what treatment, alternative source or replacement supply is appropriate.

## A storage log prevents avoidable mistakes

A simple record makes stored water easier to manage. Note the source, container type, date filled or purchased, and any treatment carried out. Keep commercially sealed water in its original packaging and follow the producer's storage advice. For water filled at home, use a container designed for drinking water, clean and sanitise it according to current public-health instructions, close it securely and mark the date. Do not reuse bottles or jugs that have held chemicals, fuel or cleaning products.

Storage conditions matter after filling. Keep containers away from direct sun, heat sources, solvents and pesticides, and place them where leaks or damage can be noticed. Inspect for cracks, loose caps, unusual appearance or odour; when in doubt, do not assume the contents remain safe. In a real incident, follow the local water supplier's exact notice. A boil-water advisory, a do-not-drink warning and a restriction caused by chemical contamination require different actions. General storage guidance is not a substitute for an active local instruction.
