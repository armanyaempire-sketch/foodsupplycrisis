Nigeria is one of the countries where acute food insecurity is deteriorating even though the national food system is far larger and more diverse than the crisis areas suggest.

The latest FAO-WFP Hunger Hotspots assessment places particular concern on the northeast, especially Borno State.

## What the latest assessment says

FAO and WFP projected around 34.8 million people in Nigeria could face Crisis or worse levels of acute food insecurity during June-August 2026.

The assessment included an estimated 15,000 people in Catastrophe in Borno.

These figures describe a defined assessment period and classification system. They should not be treated as a permanent count.

## Why Borno is vulnerable

Conflict and displacement have weakened livelihoods and market access across parts of the northeast.

Families that once relied on farming, livestock or local commerce may lose access to those income sources.

Markets can also become more expensive or difficult to reach when insecurity disrupts roads and transport.

## Why funding is important

Humanitarian operations depend on timely funding.

When resources are constrained, agencies may have to reduce the scale or coverage of assistance.

That can matter even when national food production is relatively stable because the people most at risk often cannot purchase food without support.

## Climate can add pressure

Northern Nigeria is exposed to weather variability.

Poor rainfall can reduce crop and pasture conditions. Flooding can damage fields and infrastructure.

But climate is only one part of the crisis. Conflict, displacement, poverty and market access determine whether a production shock becomes severe hunger.

## What would improve the outlook?

Immediate priorities include reliable humanitarian access, food and nutrition assistance, clean water and health services, and support for livelihoods where security allows.

Longer-term recovery depends on restoring markets, agricultural livelihoods and infrastructure.

## What readers should monitor

Watch food-security classifications, displacement, local prices, humanitarian funding, crop conditions and changes in security.

For regional context, see our [Hunger Hotspots 2026](/articles/hunger-hotspots-2026-13-countries/) article.

## Why local access and humanitarian funding matter

National agricultural output can remain substantial while communities in the northeast experience acute food insecurity. Conflict and insecurity can limit access to farmland, markets, health services and humanitarian assistance; displacement can disrupt income and social support; high food prices can make available goods unaffordable. These constraints are not interchangeable, and a national production figure alone cannot describe the situation in Borno or other affected states.

A current assessment should state which population and period it covers. If it refers to a projected lean season, do not present that number as an observed current count. If it cites a severity phase, preserve the exact IPC classification and geographic area. A projection can be urgent and useful, but it remains a forecast that should be updated when new survey and access information is available.

| Driver | What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Conflict and displacement | Recent access restrictions, displacement data and affected locations | Determines whether people can farm, work, reach markets or receive aid |
| Food prices and income | Staple prices and household purchasing power | Food may be present but unaffordable |
| Weather and production | Crop condition and regional rainfall | Affects local harvests and income, but does not explain every cause |
| Humanitarian access | Road access, security constraints and operational reach | Determines whether assistance can reach the intended population |
| Funding | Published funding appeals and gaps for the relevant response | Assistance plans cannot be assumed to be fully funded or delivered |

Follow [WFP Nigeria](https://www.wfp.org/countries/nigeria), current [IPC analyses](https://www.ipcinfo.org/) and FAO’s food-security updates. Those assessments should be used with their publication dates and specific locations. Where several organizations report the same trend, check whether they rely on the same underlying survey before treating them as fully independent evidence.

The indicators of improvement are also local: safer access to farms and markets, fewer displacement-related barriers, better household purchasing power, adequate funding and delivery of assistance, and updated assessments showing that fewer people face severe food-consumption gaps. A small change in the global food-price index would not by itself prove that conditions in an insecure district have improved.


## What an improvement would look like in the affected communities

A change in the humanitarian outlook should be assessed through the same indicators used to describe the crisis. If conflict is preventing access to farms or markets, improved access and reduced displacement barriers matter. If prices are the main constraint, household purchasing power and staple-food prices need to improve. If humanitarian funding is the limiting factor, the share of planned assistance actually delivered is a more direct measure than an announcement that a response has been funded.

A reliable update should distinguish projected food insecurity during a lean season from a count measured in a survey period. It should also keep Borno and other affected states distinct from Nigeria as a whole. Conditions in a conflict-affected district should not be inferred from national agricultural output, and a country-wide average should not erase localized severity.

For public readers, the safest way to follow new reports is to record the source, report date, geographic coverage and number or classification being quoted. Compare the new assessment with the previous edition: did the population change, did severity change, or did the report simply cover a different period? This avoids presenting revised scope as sudden improvement or deterioration.

Use the current [WFP Nigeria page](https://www.wfp.org/countries/nigeria), [IPC analyses](https://www.ipcinfo.org/) and FAO’s food-security information. A change in the global commodity index may matter to the broader economy, but it is not a direct measure of the ability of a displaced household to find food, reach a market or receive assistance. The most meaningful indicators are the ones tied to access, consumption, safety and delivery in the specific communities facing the crisis.

## Bottom line

A resilient response is simple: verify the risk, inventory what you already have, secure safe water, maintain several days of familiar shelf-stable food, protect refrigeration, rotate supplies and follow official guidance. Do not turn uncertainty into panic buying. The goal is to make a real disruption manageable while avoiding waste, financial strain and unnecessary pressure on the local food system.
