Pakistan's wheat market entered 2026 under pressure from the previous year's floods, market disruption and high flour prices.

FAO's country assessment provides a useful picture: wheat production was expected to recover with an above-average planted area, but high prices could still reduce food access for vulnerable households before the new harvest arrived.

## What happened to flour prices?

FAO reported that wheat-flour prices increased by roughly 50% to 90% in most markets between July 2025 and January 2026, reaching near-record levels.

The increase followed flood and landslide damage, lower 2025 wheat production and strong domestic demand.

That is a good example of a food-security shock caused by both physical losses and market conditions.

## Why the 2026 harvest matters

FAO estimated that the 2026 wheat area was above the five-year average.

Farmers were encouraged to plant through policy support, while soil moisture was described as adequate at planting time.

A stronger harvest can ease supply pressure.

But the effect depends on the actual yield, procurement, storage and distribution.

## Floods show why stocks matter

Agricultural disasters can destroy more than standing crops.

Floods can damage roads, warehouses, livestock, household food stores and local markets.

Even after the weather event ends, the economic damage can keep affecting prices.

## Fertilizer and farm economics

Fertilizer costs influence planting decisions and yield potential.

A farmer who faces high fertilizer costs may have to reduce application, change crop plans or delay purchases.

The effect is not guaranteed, but it can increase production risk.

## Food security is bigger than wheat

Wheat is central to the Pakistani diet, but food security also depends on income, market access, livestock, vegetables and other foods.

High wheat prices can therefore be a serious affordability issue even when the country is not physically short of every food.

## What to watch

Track:

- wheat production estimates;
- flour prices;
- government procurement;
- fertilizer availability;
- flood recovery;
- wholesale and retail spreads;
- and regional food-security assessments.

## Read flour pressure through wheat, transport and household access

Flour prices are not determined by the harvest alone. Wheat production and quality matter, but so do the location of stocks, procurement and release policy, mill costs, energy, transport, exchange rates and seasonal market arrivals. The same country can have substantial aggregate stocks and still experience local price differences when transport or distribution is uneven.

To judge whether a price movement reflects a temporary market problem or a more serious supply risk, separate four questions: Is the latest crop estimate changing? Are wheat stocks available in the relevant market? Are mills and traders receiving grain on time? And are flour prices rising in a way that households cannot absorb? Each question needs different data, and none should be inferred from a single headline.

| Indicator | What to compare | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat crop | Official estimate, baseline year, affected regions and crop stage | A preliminary forecast is not a final harvest result |
| Stocks and procurement | Dated federal/provincial stocks, procurement and releases | Stock totals may not show immediate market availability |
| Mill and retail costs | Wheat/flour prices, energy and transport | The pass-through varies by market and contract |
| Household food access | Income, food inflation and assistance availability | National supply does not guarantee affordability for every family |

For weather and agricultural context, compare Pakistan’s official crop reports with the [FAO cereal market outlook](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/csdb). For current humanitarian conditions, use the latest [WFP Pakistan information](https://www.wfp.org/countries/pakistan) and relevant IPC assessments where available. Keep dates and geographic scope visible, and distinguish official measured data from forecasts or press reports.

The practical test for a nationwide shortage is evidence of a persistent, broad constraint across production, stocks, trade and markets—not just an isolated flour-price spike. If the problem is affordability, support for vulnerable households may be more directly relevant than a claim that the country has run out of wheat. If the problem is distribution, logistics and predictable market releases deserve attention. Those distinctions make the analysis more useful to readers and less likely to amplify panic.

## Bottom line

Use the evidence in this article to judge whether the risk is real and local. Prepare proportionately: secure water, keep a modest rotating food buffer, protect refrigeration, follow official guidance and avoid panic buying.
