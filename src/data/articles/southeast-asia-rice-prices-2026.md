> **Update — 19 November 2026:** Replaced the generic crisis appendix with a rice-market comparison that keeps paddy, milled-rice, export and retail measures separate.

Rice is a useful commodity for understanding how climate risk, stocks, production and prices can move in different directions.

Southeast Asia is central to the rice trade, and the late-2026 El Niño pattern is increasing attention on rainfall, irrigation and the 2027 crop.

## Indonesia: high stocks, high prices

Reuters reported that Indonesian retail rice prices reached 15,769 rupiah per kilogram in September 2026.

At the same time, Indonesia's 2026 rice production is projected at about 34 million tonnes, above consumption.

That looks contradictory until the market is examined more closely.

Production can be sufficient while retail prices remain high because of procurement costs, distribution problems, price ceilings, location and inventory policy.

## Why the 2027 harvest matters

The bigger concern is the next production cycle.

Reuters reported that Indonesia's 2027 output could fall by as much as 10% under severe El Niño-related drought conditions.

About 30% of paddy fields lack proper irrigation, increasing sensitivity to a shorter or weaker rainy season.

A forecast is not a confirmed crop loss, but it can influence prices before planting and harvest outcomes are known.

## Thailand and the export market

Thailand is an important rice exporter, so changes in Thai prices can affect importing countries.

When buyers anticipate tighter regional supply, export quotations can rise even before a physical shortage appears.

That is how expectation becomes part of commodity pricing.

## Stocks do not automatically lower retail prices

Strategic stocks can help stabilize markets, but their effect depends on when and how they are released.

If the private distribution network faces higher costs, public stocks may stabilize availability without returning retail prices to their previous level.

## What El Niño adds

A strong El Niño can alter rainfall across Southeast Asia.

The effect is not identical in every country or every province.

Irrigation infrastructure is therefore crucial. A farm with reliable irrigation may withstand dry conditions better than a rain-fed farm.

## What to watch

For a comparable rice-market series, record:

- rice retail prices;
- export quotations;
- rainfall and irrigation;
- crop planting conditions;
- government reserve releases;
- and 2027 production forecasts.



## Bottom line

For Southeast Asia, compare rice prices only after matching grade, unit, shipment term and market stage. Paddy production, milled-rice output, export quotations and retail prices describe different parts of the supply chain and should not be ranked in one undifferentiated table.

## Rice comparisons need a unit, crop year and market label

Rice statistics are easy to misread when the unit is omitted. Paddy or rough rice is not the same product as milled rice: milling removes husk and bran and changes the quantity available for consumption. A national paddy estimate should therefore not be compared directly with a milled-rice production or trade figure. The source, conversion basis and crop year need to be stated.

The same discipline applies across Southeast Asia. A Thai export quotation, an Indonesian paddy harvest estimate, a Vietnamese export offer and a Philippine retail price are different stages of the market. They can move in different directions because harvest timing, domestic procurement, exchange rates, export policy and local distribution vary. A regional average can conceal a country-specific problem.

For a useful comparison, record the commodity form, unit, reference period, geography and price level for every figure. Then ask whether the change is in production, exports, imports or retail affordability. This prevents a large paddy number from being mistaken for immediately available milled rice, and stops an export-price movement from being presented as a direct measure of what consumers pay.

## Export quotations and domestic rice prices answer different questions

A rice export quotation is typically a price for a specified origin, grade, shipment window and delivery term. It cannot be treated as the price paid by households in another country. Importers add freight, insurance, port charges, financing, handling, taxes and domestic distribution; exchange rates can change the local-currency cost even when the dollar quotation is stable. Domestic procurement rules or retail support programmes can create another gap between wholesale and consumer prices.

When comparing Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia and the Philippines, state the grade and delivery basis wherever possible. Keep exporter offers separate from importer tender results and retail price series. Also distinguish the domestic role of rice: a country may be a major exporter while maintaining a separate domestic price policy, or an importer may buy at a favourable tender price but pass it through slowly. A table that gives the unit, grade, date and market stage is far more useful than one that simply ranks countries by “rice prices.”
