> **Update — 19 November 2026:** Added a U.S. regional crop-and-drought guide using the 10 November Drought Monitor and USDA's 15 November winter-wheat progress; farm advice now distinguishes Plains conditions from local consumer-price questions.

The 2026 food-price story in the United States is not just about what appears on a supermarket shelf.

Farm economics can change long before consumers see a price tag move. Fertilizer, fuel, transportation, crop yields, processing costs and global commodity markets all influence the final cost of food.

Fertilizer is one of the upstream risks getting extra attention this year.

## Why fertilizer matters to US farmers

Fertilizer is a major input for crop production, especially nitrogen fertilizer.

When fertilizer becomes more expensive or harder to obtain, farmers face difficult decisions about how much to apply and when to buy it.

The effect is not identical across crops or farms.

Soil conditions, crop prices and expected yields all influence how a producer responds.

## What USDA announced

In July 2026, USDA announced the FIELDS program, with up to $500 million available to expand domestic fertilizer production capacity and strengthen the U.S. fertilizer supply chain.

The program is designed to support new or expanded facilities, competition and supply resilience.

That is a long-term supply response. It does not mean current fertilizer costs disappear immediately.

## How fertilizer reaches the grocery bill

The pathway is usually indirect.

Higher fertilizer costs can raise farm production costs.

Farmers may try to absorb those costs, reduce other spending or change input decisions.

If many producers face the same pressure, expectations for future crop costs can change.

Processors, livestock producers and traders can also face secondary effects.

Eventually, some of those costs can reach food prices.

But the transmission is not one-for-one.

## Why a fertilizer increase does not equal a food shortage

The United States has substantial agricultural production and access to international trade.

That provides buffers.

Farmers can also adjust planting, application, sourcing and storage.

A fertilizer shock is therefore a cost and production risk rather than proof that American food supplies are about to disappear.

## What consumers should look at

For household food costs, follow domestic food inflation rather than relying only on fertilizer headlines.

Also watch:

- grain prices;
- livestock feed costs;
- diesel and energy;
- crop forecasts;
- import costs;
- and retailer pricing.

These indicators together give a more realistic view of grocery pressure.

## Why global markets still matter

US agriculture is connected to world markets.

Changes in Black Sea grain flows can affect global wheat and maize prices.

Energy disruptions can alter fertilizer costs.

Weather events in major producing regions can change export demand.

That means domestic food prices can respond to events thousands of miles away.

## What is the long-term issue?

The bigger policy question is supply resilience.

Domestic fertilizer production can reduce dependence on some international supply chains, while competition and diversified sourcing can reduce concentration risk.

But fertilizer independence is not the same as food-price independence.

US farms still operate within global commodity, energy and logistics markets.


## Bottom line

The US food-price risk in 2026 is best understood as a chain of costs rather than one shortage story.

Fertilizer can influence farm economics. Energy and transport influence distribution. Global crop and commodity markets influence costs.

The useful signal for consumers is not one dramatic headline, but whether several of those costs remain elevated at the same time.

## Why a change in farm costs is not the same as a grocery-price forecast

US food prices reflect several stages between a farm and a household. Fertilizer, seed, fuel, machinery and labour affect production costs, but their effect on retail prices depends on the crop, the share of the final product represented by the raw commodity, processing and transport costs, contracts, inventories and competition among suppliers. A cost increase for farmers may first reduce margins rather than immediately raise the price on a supermarket shelf.

Different foods also respond differently. Grain and oilseed markets are exposed to harvest forecasts and export demand; meat and dairy prices depend on feed costs and biological production cycles; fresh produce is more sensitive to seasonality, weather and spoilage during transport. A national food CPI combines many categories, so it can move differently from a single commodity benchmark.

To interpret a US price change, compare the same reference period across USDA farm and wholesale indicators, BLS food-at-home and food-away-from-home CPI, and the relevant commodity series. Keep annual inflation separate from monthly movement, and do not describe a fertilizer-price change as evidence of a nationwide food shortage.

## Regional conditions matter more than a national fertilizer headline

The U.S. drought picture is mixed. Drought.gov's 4–10 November summary reported improvement in parts of Texas and the Interior West, while drought worsened in eastern Oklahoma and Texas, the Lower Mississippi Basin and parts of the Great Lakes. USDA's 15 November crop report also showed winter-wheat emergence below the five-year average in Oklahoma (66% versus 79%), while Kansas was close to average (86% versus 85%) and Texas was slightly ahead (74% versus 71%). These are different regional signals, not one national crop condition.

| Region or audience | What to check before acting |
|---|---|
| Southern Plains winter-wheat growers | Compare county soil moisture, the U.S. Drought Monitor's short- and long-term impact categories and state extension guidance. In Oklahoma, where emergence remained below its five-year average, check stand establishment before deciding on replanting or additional inputs. Do not raise fertilizer rates to compensate for poor emergence without a field assessment. |
| Kansas and Nebraska wheat growers | Use the state crop-progress series and local soil-moisture reports; statewide emergence was close to or above the five-year average in the latest USDA table. A national drought headline should not be used to infer that every Plains field needs the same response. |
| Grain handlers and elevators | Set receiving limits by measured grain moisture and drying throughput, not by silo nameplate capacity. Where wet harvest conditions occur, segregate lots and keep aeration and pest monitoring operational. |
| Households and retailers | Compare the local BLS food-at-home categories and city-level prices with the actual basket sold in the area. A national fertilizer or grain benchmark does not tell a shopper whether a particular town is facing a delivery delay or a retail-price increase. |

The Drought Monitor is a broad-scale product; local conditions can vary within a state. Farmers should use their county extension office, state climatologist and field measurements for field-level decisions.
